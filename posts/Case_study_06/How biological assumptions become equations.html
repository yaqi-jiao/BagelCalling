Case

This case is from Neuronal activity and amyloid-β promote tau seeding in the entorhinal cortex in Alzheimer’s disease.

The reason why I saved this paper as a case study is that it has a detailed and well-organized Methods section, which makes it a good study material for understanding how a simulation model is constructed. In particular, because this paper is not simply modelling the conventional prion-like spread of tau across the whole disease course, the authors have to explain clearly why they chose this particular model structure.

When I took Modeling System Analysis (BIOS13), the professor said that simulation models are built based on biological assumptions, and that we define parameters to represent different processes. At that time, I did not fully understand what this meant. This paper gave me a much clearer example.

Another reason is that, when I see equations in papers, I often feel intimidated because I imagine that the authors somehow invented the equations from scratch based on mathematical knowledge that I do not have. This paper helped reveal what actually happens behind the equations.

The question I really wanted this paper to answer was: Did they invent all of this themselves?!!?

Model scope

The choice of model depends on the research question and on which biological processes are necessary to answer it. In this paper, the authors are not modelling tau pathology across the entire course of AD. Their question is narrower: at a very early stage, before widespread pathology is established,

which brain regions are most susceptible to tau seed accumulation, and why is the entorhinal cortex particularly vulnerable?

They specifically ask whether regional neuronal activity or amyloid-β deposition can provide a mechanistic explanation for this vulnerability.

Baseline framework

The authors assume that, at this very early stage, tau seed concentrations are low enough that nonlinear processes such as strong local prion-like amplification are not yet dominant. Therefore, they use a linear diffusion model as the main framework.

As they explain:

In this study, however, we are not modelling the progression of tau pathology throughout the entire disease but rather the diffusion of early tau seeds before disease initiation. Our goal was to predict which regions are most susceptible to early tau seeding. Since protein densities are low in the early stage, non-linear effects do not yet play a role and linear effects dominate.

The baseline model describes the transport/diffusion of tau seeds among brain regions.

Brain is represented as a graph

  • brain regions are the nodes
  • structural connections between regions are represented by the weighted adjacency matrix \(W\).

Tau seed concentration is represented by \(u\).

Tau production and degradation rates are assumed to be the same across regions, because regional differences in production and degradation are not the main research question here.

Standard network diffusion is represented using the graph Laplacian \(L\). This is a commonly used operator in network diffusion models, not a model parameter.

The authors then modify this baseline transport process using a regional anomaly level \(A_i\) derived from either:

  • neuronal activity, measured using FDG PET;
  • amyloid deposition, measured using Aβ PET

The central biological hypothesis is therefore:

  • neuronal activity can modulate tau transport;
  • amyloid deposition can modulate tau transport.

The strength of additional transport effect is controlled by \(ε\)

Set the parameter

\(u˙i=−ρj∑Lij(1+εAj)uj+k−λui\)

ε controls the strength of the PET-derived modulation of tau transport。

The core model-specific mechanistic construction is: \(1+εAi\)

The authors did not fit these parameters to tau PET data. Instead, they manually chose a parameter regime in which transport dominates over local production. They then investigated how changing ε affected the results as a sensitive analysis

Evaluate model performance

The authors evaluate the model in several ways:

  • the model prediction agrees with biological sense (especially in relation fo early Braak stages) or not

  • null model to avoid result by chance

  • compare model-derived prediction with empirical tau PET data

  • this is the part i found interesting: covariate adjustment regression: they used model-derived entorhinal seeding as one predictor, together with age, sex, and APOE4 carrier status, and used empirical entorhinal tau PET as the response:

    \(tau=β0+β1(model prediction)+β2age+β3sex+β4APOE4\)

    This is a multiple linear regression rather than a fixed-effects model.

  • replicate in an independent cohort

Follow-up statistical analysis

I think this section can be connected with our previous case study about choosing a statistical model based on the question being asked. Statistical analysis is not only applied to raw biological data; it can also be applied to model outputs.

  • to assess group difference: Subjects are stratified in two different ways: by clinical diagnosis (CN, MCI, AD) and by biomarker status (Aβ/tau groups).
  • to asses the significance of result seed region: they built a null model to avoid random chance.
  • to assess correlation: this step is more likely a model performance check

Study

I want to compare this model with Zheng Ying-Qing’s original SIR paper, which is also the basis of my current project. But Zheng’s implementation is specifically an agent-based SIR-style model, whereas Alexandersen’s model represents tau as a continuous regional concentration. So not all connectome-based mechanistic models use the same mathematical formalism.

What they do share at a high level is that the brain is represented as a network, with structural connectivity encoded by an adjacency matrix \(W\). But even the way \(W\) enters the model can be different

A general process for mechanistic model

  1. Define biological question

  2. Define how the real system will be compressed into model variables.

  3. Translate biological assumptions into mathematical terms or simulation rules

  4. Choose the mathematical formalism.

    This is probably the most important concept for me. More details are listed in another post!

  5. Determine how parameters will be obtained. Parameters may be:

    1. known or externally measured
    2. estimated/fitted from empirical data;
    3. manually chosen to explore a particular parameter regime

    Sensitivity analysis is a separate step: it asks whether conclusions remain stable when parameters are changed

  6. parameter fitting - For unknown parameters, empirical data can be used to find parameter values that make model output agree with observations, for example by minimizing MSE

    problem: parameter identifiability, different parameter combinations may sometimes produce very similar model behaviur

  7. model analysis doesn’t always mean simulation — in my course, it’s steady state and stability. It may also include threshold, bifurcation, eigenmodes. again so many new things. but I’ll keep them zipped at least for now and study them when needed.

  8. model validation

  9. null model / control model: e.g. shuffle / permutation null, or simpler baseline model, or remove one mechanism

  10. model interface and causality.

    IMPORTANT: a mechanistic model fitting empirical data does not mean that the proposed causal chain must be true in the real biological system. Stronger causal support usually comes from perturbation experiments, interventions, longitudinal evidence, and independent mechanistic measurements. Therefore, conclusions should distinguish: “the data are consistent with this mechanism” from “this mechanism has been causally demonstrated.”

  11. Interesting thing: Mechanistic and data-driven approaches can complement each other.
